# NBD: Rationale and Alternatives

This document is informative. Requirements are in [Core](core.md), [Device Enrollment](device-enrollment.md), and [Auditing](audit.md).

## Why this design

NBD targets retained shared data in atproto Spaces. Current members may read pre-join history; applications create spaces frequently and reuse device trust across them. The host already controls account membership. Signed approvals make an app installation one of an account's devices in all its spaces, while any eligible holder can distribute keys through its own repo without an appointed issuer.

NBD uses trust on first use only at first contact with an account: it accepts PDS key-substitution risk there and relies on conditional later discovery through published grants and direct repo sync. Later devices need an approval from a device the client already accepted. Accepting unapproved key changes with a notice would have kept total device loss recoverable, but it made linking optional: a compromised PDS could add a device through the same path as legitimate recovery, and that device would receive all history before anyone could intervene. Requiring approval makes linking a real boundary. Independent device verification would prevent first-contact substitution, but it is deferred. With one shared key, the weakest exchange sets a whole space's protection, so verification helps only if every exchange in a space requires it; a verified pair inside a TOFU space gains nothing. Supporting verified-only and TOFU spaces side by side, over device trust shared across spaces, also produced cross-mode failures in earlier drafts: a device accepted through an unapproved key change could revoke a verified device everywhere. One trust mode removes that class of error along with the verification ceremony, disputes, and per-mode bookkeeping. The cost is that NBD offers high-risk groups no protection against a compromised or compelled PDS at first contact beyond detection. Grant evidence records the actual recipient selection and decision, while normal Spaces access governs who can audit it.

One key for the life of a space makes all retained history available through one grant per device. NBD does not [rotate](core.md#no-rotation) that key. Spaces already give the host access control over who can fetch ciphertext, so removal relies on it: a removed participant keeps the key, and keeping later content from them depends on the host. That requires a read policy that authenticates users, and for a removed device it also requires the user to cut the device off at their PDS, since the host grants access to accounts rather than devices ([ending access](core.md#ending-access)). Rotation would add protection only against a host that misbehaves, at the cost of epochs, history wrapping, carried-forward exclusion state, host activation, and writer pauses. Removal therefore needs no new space. Spaces are cheap to create, so users who credibly believe the host or PDSes are compromised in a way access control cannot stop can [replace the space](core.md#replacing-a-space). These choices trade stronger secrecy and freshness guarantees for history access, asynchronous operation, and simplicity. Key compromise exposes the whole space, revocation takes effect only when learned, and a lost key loses the space. Trust in the host and application software remains essential.

Devices are enrolled account-wide rather than per space. Per-space selection could not contain a compromised installation, which can approve devices and join spaces without remote validators seeing whether the user confirmed, and it cost per-space declaration histories and conflicts. Containing applications is left to host membership and the PDS's repo permissions; request-driven delivery keeps honest applications from holding keys they do not use.

Devices add; removal happens in the account's repo. Earlier drafts let devices sign revocations, and kept a revoked device's authority over devices accepted before its revocation so that delivery order could not change the outcome. A stolen device could then revoke every surviving device, and with approval required, the account could never add a device again on clients that had pinned it. Revoking by deleting the device record moves that authority to whoever can write the record, and NBD applications hold only create-only sessions, so a stolen installation cannot delete or overwrite its peers' records on a PDS that enforces OAuth permissions. NBD cannot change PDS behavior, so app passwords, broad-scope applications, and the account password still can; their damage is recoverable through re-enrollment. Removal also needs no ordering: a binding is revoked once any verified commit shows it absent, so there are no competing statements to rank, and the cutoff rule goes. Removal alone does not give clients a shared history: repo commits do not chain, so a malicious PDS can show different clients different histories. [Relay-witnessed history](#relay-witnessed-history) supplies one. Absence is proven by enumerating the repo at a signed commit no older than the client's last observation, because atproto keeps no tombstones. In exchange, a PDS can revoke every device and re-enroll one, as legitimate recovery after total device loss does. A mandatory hold, surfaced to members and to the account's own installations, lets users stop that when the PDS is honest but their account was taken over. The hold protects only clients that knew the account before the reset. Later drafts tried to protect newcomers too, with anchors: bindings witnessed by grants from members the newcomer had already accepted, scoped per space and then account-wide. Each version gained rules, and review kept composing first-contact substitution through the witnesses into wider compromise; one malicious PDS hosting two accounts could manufacture its own witness. NBD instead accepted first-contact exposure, then narrowed it without witnesses: under [grant standing](#inviters-and-grant-standing) only the inviter's own first contact is exposed, as a new contact of a hijacked phone number is on Signal. The analogy stops there; Signal's ratchets also limit what a compromised key exposes, and NBD's one space key does not. The [model comparison](model/COMPARISON.md) records the alternatives. Against a malicious PDS, which can hide the deletions from the user's installations, the hold leaves detection, the same protection as first contact; relay-witnessed history makes that detection dependable.

## Inviters and grant standing

The re-enrollment hold first protected only clients that knew an account before its reset. With one shared key, a single member meeting the account for the first time after a takeover, whether a newcomer, a member whose app synced late, or one who met the account in another space, could grant the attacker's device the key at once and undo the hold for everyone. [Grant standing](device-enrollment.md#grant-standing) closes that without witnesses: every client holds a device it accepted for a first-contact hold as long as the re-enrollment hold, except in spaces the member's own account [invites](core.md#invites) the account into, where it may grant at once to devices it met on first use. Nobody trusts anyone else's judgement, so there is nothing for an attacker to compose through. The inviter's first contact happens at a moment a person chose, which is also where verification would go: under this rule, inviters verifying their invitees would protect a space, rather than every pair of members.

**Alternatives.** A blanket first-contact hold for everyone, inviters included, closes the gap too, but makes every new space wait three days for anyone new. Space admins, starting from the creator, who alone may grant at first contact were also designed. With members inviting, an admin's app could meet an invitee long after the invite, leaving a window for a takeover between them, and roles brought governance and availability costs that casual spaces should not carry. Inviters needs no roles and no host involvement: the inviter is established by its own signed record, so a malicious host cannot make an honest member skip its holds.

**Costs.** A new member gets the key from their inviter's devices, or later from members whose own holds have ended and who have a device running, with no time anyone can promise. A new space with someone the creator knew who reset their devices in the last few days waits for the re-enrollment hold, which is the takeover protection doing its job. The client trust logic roughly doubles, nearly all of it in invite lifecycle: durable preparation, pending invites, abandonment, and transfers between a user's installations.

**How the rule was reached.** It was reviewed five times, each round modeled in the [Python model](model/README.md#what-the-inviters-variant-found), the [prototype](prototype/README.md), and the [Tamarin model](formal/README.md):

1. The first draft let a vouch count in every space, so an inviter in one space could disclose another space's key; vouches became scoped. An unverified "let them in now" override was dropped, and no unlock time is promised. The Python model then found that first-contact holds had to start on approval as well as first use, and that revocation had to freeze credit rather than erase it.
2. Inviting someone before they installed meant a three-day wait, a failed write could silently drop the vouch, and a second space with the same person waited: these brought pending invites, durable invites, and vouches that follow the inviter's later invites. Tamarin proved scope survives approval chains.
3. That last change combined two independent facts, a vouch and any past admission, so a later vouch, even one made by another of the inviter's installations, retroactively authorized an older invite. Every check had verified the same two facts. Records now carry an `authorizes` list fixed when they are made, and the checks trace every authorization to a specific record; Tamarin finds the attack when the rule is removed.
4. An abandoned pending invite could still capture, the prototype saved the vouch only on publication, and a replacement phone lost its standing in the next space: abandonment now cancels pending state and queued captures, and records authorize devices linked from vouched ones.
5. A stale import could resurrect an abandoned pending invite, and adoption and imports still required a direct vouch for replacements: pending state no longer transfers, abandonment does, and transfers apply the descendant rule.

6. After adoption, the rule that an invite authorizes only devices first met through an invite turned out to make a new space wait whenever its creator had met someone some other way, such as a fellow member of another space, in the last three days: knowing someone slightly gave a worse experience than not knowing them at all. Its reason, that re-inviting must not override a running hold, had already been covered by invites admitting only non-members. Invites now authorize any device the inviter met on first use, whenever and however it met it, and never a reset's new devices; records are still fixed when made.

The findings narrowed each round, from a design flaw to lifecycle edge cases. The one serious regression came from a convenience that relied on two separately checked facts without tying them together, which is worth watching for when the rule is extended.

## Relay-witnessed history

NBD's answer to first-contact substitution is that the account holder finds out. Before relay witnessing, that held only for a takeover at an honest PDS. A malicious PDS could show the inviter's client an impostor's device record and show its own user an unchanged repo, since commits do not chain, and the user's remaining signal, the grant in the inviter's space repo, sat behind three things the PDS controls: the delegation token that gates the user's access to every space, the paths by which the user learns that a space exists, and, when the inviter shared the PDS, the grant itself. A PDS hiding a reset from its own user was the same trick. [Witnessed history](device-enrollment.md#witnessed-history) closes all of these with records that are already public: relays are common infrastructure, run apart from PDSes, so once every client judges device records against the reference relay's one history, an impostor any honest client accepts appears in, or leaves a gap in, the history the account's own installations read ([own-account history](audit.md#own-account-history)). (These relay witnesses are unrelated to the anchor witnesses rejected above.)

A signed commit proves what the repo said, not that the relay broadcast it, so publication is vouched for by a party the client already trusts: its application's witness. NBD already trusts applications with installation keys, so the witness adds no new party, though as an online service it is a different target from shipped code, and detection needs both the user's witness and the accepting client's witness honest.

**Alternatives.**
- **Warning before disclosure.** An objection window on invites was set aside. The parties who could object are the ones the attacker has cut off: under a malicious PDS the objection goes through that PDS; after a takeover the old installations have lost their device records and likely their sessions; for a pending invite no honest device exists yet. A window would also delay every invite, the blanket hold rejected [above](#inviters-and-grant-standing). Independent [verification](TODOs.md#deferred-extensions) is the real fix.
- **Public contact records.** Clients would publish a public record naming each device they accepted at first contact, confirm it reached an index the PDS does not run, and only then grant; installations would look their own account up daily. It closed the same gaps, but publicly revealed who shares encrypted spaces with whom, and added a record type, a confirmation step, and an index dependency.
- **Liveness on space access only.** A warning when an installation cannot sync its spaces, plus a daily sweep, turns a blocked space into a visible warning, but leaves spaces the user never learned of, and a shared PDS, out of reach.

**Costs.** The reference relay joins the trust model, and naming one concentrates trust in its operator. If the relay or a witness is down or lagging, clients cannot accept or remove devices until it recovers, though existing trust and grants are unaffected. Every application runs a witness that tails the full firehose, reconstructs two collections for every account, fetches repos on `#sync` events and after gaps, and retains 90 days of changes, and accepting a device waits seconds for the relay and the witness. Warnings carry a habituation cost: a PDS can create gaps at will, and an honest witness outage longer than the relay's replay window warns every user of that application, so harmless gaps can train users to acknowledge the one that hides an impostor. A PDS can also avoid the unrelayed-commit alarm by following a hidden commit with a `#sync` or misstating the next commit's unsigned `since`; the hidden commit is still never accepted.

**How the rule was reached.** It was reviewed six times, each round modeled in the [Python model](model/README.md), the [prototype](prototype/README.md), and the [Tamarin model](formal/README.md):

1. A client could take any source's word that a commit was published, so a PDS acting as a witness could supply signed commits the relay never carried; clients now trust only their application's witness. A `#sync` carries only a commit, so reconstruction became exact-root, with permanent gaps, and restores resume from their saved cursor.
2. A ping-based notion of a live witness let an honest relay's backlog hide an impostor for longer than the deadline; liveness is measured by the relay's own event times instead, after a relay-head request turned out not to exist. A witness that started following an account late hid what came before it, which Tamarin found as an attack; missing history is a gap. Cursors became repo revisions, so installations move between witnesses.
3. The unrelayed-commit alarm fired on commits an honest relay drops; it was narrowed to consecutive reconstructions, and revisions far in the future became gaps, not alarms.
4. Returning to the same revision after an outage proved nothing, since a relay that passes on non-increasing revisions lets a PDS replay one over an impostor; stream gaps reach past the resume. Event times had to be the relay's own, and coverage ages from the request, not the response.
5. The same replay defeated a rebuilt witness, whose starting revision bounded nothing, and changes that cancel out still false-alarmed; rebuild gaps are bounded like stream gaps, and consecutiveness checks `since` as well as the root.
6. Relay times are not unique, so the startup check orders by relay sequence number, and an honest `#sync` re-announcing the current commit no longer alarms.

The findings moved from trust (who vouches for publication) to timing (what "caught up" means) to bookkeeping at the edges of a history (where it starts, what a gap covers), and nearly every late finding came from an assumption that one value bounded another: a resync revision, a starting revision, a timestamp. That is the pattern to check when the rule is extended.

## Comparison

The comparison concerns key mechanisms and their integration, rather than every feature of the products using them.

| Mechanism | Relevant properties | Relationship to NBD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [MLS, RFC 9420](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9420.html) | Group epochs, authenticated updates, and tree-based key establishment provide forward secrecy and post-compromise security under the protocol's assumptions. Joining bootstraps the new epoch; its Welcome is not an archive of prior epochs. | A strong candidate when those guarantees are required. Spaces would still need membership/identity integration and a history-sharing layer; NBD currently chooses one retained space key and host authority instead of MLS group state. |
| [Megolm](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/olm-megolm/megolm/) | Each sender has an outbound ratchet and signing key. Retaining/exporting an earlier ratchet point permits reading from that point onward. Retained history limits forward secrecy; compromise exposes later messages in that session until a new session replaces it. Session-key exchange depends on a secure channel. | Closer to retained-history messaging, but adds per-sender session distribution and ratchet state. NBD uses one content key per space epoch without adding [message-authorship proofs](core.md#encrypted-records). |
| [Signal sender keys](https://github.com/signalapp/libsignal/blob/main/rust/protocol/src/group_cipher.rs) | libsignal derives message keys from per-sender chain state, signs messages, and supplies distribution-message operations. Membership authorization, delivery of distribution messages, and archive policy require surrounding application logic. | Sender-key mechanics alone do not supply NBD's enrollment or grant audit contract. Adopting them would change content/state handling; this comparison does not equate sender keys with every security property of the Signal application or its pairwise protocol. |
| [Keybase team keys](https://book.keybase.io/docs/teams/crypto) | Team generations distribute a seed through per-user keys; team signing chains record public keys. Rotation wraps the previous seed under material derived from the next generation, making history accessible to new members. | The closest history-distribution precedent. NBD drops generations entirely, keeping one key per space, with direct grants to selected installation keys, host membership decisions, and per-space declarations instead of importing Keybase's per-user key and team-signing-chain architecture. |

## Why not MLS now?

### The requirements diverge where MLS is strongest

MLS is designed so that compromise of a member's state exposes as little past and future traffic as possible. NBD's central requirement runs the other way: current members, including newly joined devices, read the space's retained history. Any device able to read that history exposes it if compromised, whatever key-agreement protocol delivered its keys. For retained Spaces content, MLS's forward secrecy therefore protects little beyond the key-agreement state itself.

History access does not rule out MLS. The RFC defines [exporters](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9420.html#section-8.5) for application secrets. An application could keep separately retained archive keys and send them to new members over MLS; that is an architectural option inferred from these facilities, not an archive feature promised by the RFC. Doing so means rebuilding most of NBD on top of MLS:

| NBD component | Still required with MLS? |
| --- | --- |
| A retained [space key](core.md#key-generation) | Yes. MLS epoch secrets are deleted as the group advances, and a Welcome bootstraps only the current epoch. Pre-join history needs a separate retained key with its own compromise semantics. |
| Host [membership](core.md#trusted-host-state) | Yes. Spaces already gives the host membership authority, and MLS needs a delivery service to order commits. Clients could refuse additions no member committed, but that would contradict Spaces administration rather than reduce host trust in practice. |
| [Approvals](device-enrollment.md#approvals), [local trust](device-enrollment.md#local-trust), and [revocation](device-enrollment.md#device-revocations) | Yes. MLS authenticates leaves through credentials whose validation the application defines. Mapping atproto accounts to app installations, authenticating bindings, and making revocation permanent across spaces stay application work. |
| [Grant evidence](audit.md#evidence) and [auditing](audit.md) | Yes, or a replacement. The MLS transcript authenticates group operations to members, but it is not repo-published evidence of who released which keys under which authorization. |
| [Content encryption](core.md#content-encryption) | Mostly. Retained records and blobs need keys that outlive MLS epochs, so content would be encrypted under the archive keys rather than MLS application messages. |

What MLS would replace is the narrowest part of NBD: [delivering the space key to members' devices](core.md#key-grants). NBD does that with one HPKE grant per device.

### Costs MLS adds for Spaces

- **Per-space group state.** Applications create spaces frequently. Each would be an MLS group whose ratchet tree every device stores and updates. An NBD device needs only its keys and the grants addressed to it.
- **Ordered processing.** MLS members process commits in epoch order. An offline device must replay every missed commit, or rejoin, before it can follow the group, and lost state is hard to recover. NBD grants can be read independently and in any order; one grant recovers the whole space.
- **Epoch churn from device changes.** Adding or removing a device in MLS requires a commit that advances the epoch for every member, in every space the device joins. In NBD, neither additions nor removals change the key; a new device simply receives grants.
- **Key package supply.** Joining via Welcome consumes a key package that is generally single-use. Devices would publish and replenish key packages in their repos, adding availability and exhaustion cases that NBD's long-lived published device keys avoid.

### What NBD gives up

MLS brings standardized security analysis and stronger guarantees that NBD explicitly does not provide. The most relevant loss is post-compromise security for device keys. In NBD, an attacker who silently copies a device's encryption key can decrypt every later grant to that device until the binding is revoked, and anyone who copies a space key can read that space for as long as they can obtain its ciphertext. MLS heals this once the device sends an update; NBD's only remedy is a new space. MLS removal also excludes a removed member cryptographically, where NBD relies on the host.

The [executable model](model/README.md) and [prototype](prototype/README.md) test the enrollment and grant rules, including under partial views and malicious-PDS behavior; the [Tamarin model](formal/README.md) adds symbolic proofs of grant and approval authenticity, key agreement, and space-key secrecy under device compromise and a malicious PDS, abstracting time and repo mechanics. Simplicity is an objective, not a demonstrated advantage: NBD avoids group-ratchet state but introduces enrollment, revocation, recovery, and audit machinery that requires independent review. A comparison implementation, very large spaces, or a requirement for post-compromise security would justify revisiting this choice. A middle option would use MLS only to deliver NBD's epoch keys while keeping history wrapping and host authority. Periodic device encryption-key rotation could narrow the post-compromise gap without MLS.
